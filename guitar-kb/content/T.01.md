### One fret = one half step
Every fret raises the pitch by one **semitone** (half step). Two frets = a **whole step**. An **interval** is just the distance between two notes, counted in frets.

| Frets | Interval | Sound / use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | minor 2nd | tense, the "Jaws" step |
| 2 | major 2nd | one step of a scale |
| 3 | minor 3rd | the sad-sounding 3rd |
| 4 | major 3rd | the happy-sounding 3rd |
| 5 | perfect 4th | "Here comes the bride" |
| 6 | tritone | unstable, wants to resolve |
| 7 | perfect 5th | power chords, strong and open |
| 8 | minor 6th | |
| 9 | major 6th | sweet; double stops in worship leads |
| 10 | minor 7th | the "7" in G7 |
| 11 | major 7th | the dreamy "maj7" |
| 12 | octave | same note, higher |

### Shortcuts on the neck
- **Same fret, next string up = a 4th** (5 frets) on every string pair except G→B.
- **G→B is a major 3rd**, so any shape that crosses from string 3 to string 2 shifts **up one fret**. That's why chord and scale shapes have that kink.
- **Two frets up, next string = a 5th.** That's the power chord shape.
- **Two frets up, two strings over = an octave** (strings 6→4 and 5→3). From string 4 or 3, it's three frets (because of G→B).
